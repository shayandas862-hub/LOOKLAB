## 1. Shot generation (text-to-video, image-to-video, video-to-video): who leads today?

### Takeaway
As of mid-October 2026 the human-preference top tier is crowded and statistically tied. On Artificial Analysis (AA), **Alibaba Wan 3.0** leads text-to-video with audio (Elo 1174) and **MiniMax H3 / Seedance 2.5 / Gemini Omni Flash 1.1 / Wan 3.0** are tied for image-to-video. The arena.ai snapshot (search summary only) puts **Google Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash** first. Most top models were released in July–September 2026. OpenAI's Sora 2 API was switched off on 2026-09-24. Leaders change every few months, and a vendor can withdraw a model with six months' notice.

### Inferences
- No single model "leads". The top 4–6 on each AA board have overlapping confidence intervals. Google, Alibaba, ByteDance and MiniMax are the four vendors to watch for quality, and Wan 3.0 and Gemini Omni Flash are the strongest on price for their quality (about $0.10–0.20/s).
- Model churn is fast and a vendor can withdraw a model: Sora 2 went from a 2025 launch to API removal by September 2026. An agent that is not tied to any one model (route through Runway or fal, or keep its own adapter layer) is protected against this. The client's skeleton is model-agnostic, which helps here.
- For a UK-facing business, several leaders come with regional restrictions: MiniMax H3 open weights require a licence application in the UK; Gemini Omni will not edit or extend uploaded videos in the UK; Veo limits person generation in the UK. These matter directly to a "render, then video-to-video" pipeline.
- Lengths are still short: 8 s for Veo, 15 s for Kling and H3, 30 s for Wan 3.0 and Seedance 2.5. A finished film is still an edit of many shots, which supports the client's plan of building per-shot skeletons.

## 2. Control and consistency: same character/product/logo, structure control, camera control, exact text and logos

### Takeaway
Identity references are now standard in the top closed models: Veo up to 3 images, H3 up to 9, Seedance 2.5 up to 50 files, Gemini Omni multiple images plus video clips, Runway Characters and Brand Kits. **Dense structure control** (depth, edges, segmentation, pose maps) is mostly found in **open or research models**: Wan 2.1 VACE, NVIDIA Cosmos-Transfer, Marey's pose and trajectory controls. The top closed models offer the looser path of **video-to-video restyle**: Runway Aleph 2, Luma Modify with "strong" adherence, Seedance 2.5 reference video, Gemini Omni editing. Camera control in the top closed models is mostly prompt or keyframe based, not numerical. No vendor publishes measured failure rates for keeping a product, logo or on-screen text exact, and I found no tool that claims pixel-exact product preservation.

### Inferences
- There is a split between quality and control. The models that win human-preference votes (Wan 3.0, Seedance 2.5, Gemini Omni, H3) expose references, keyframes and video-to-video, but **no documented depth, edge or segmentation control inputs** on the pages read. The models with dense control inputs (Wan 2.1 VACE, Cosmos-Transfer2.5, LTX-2.x) rank lower or are older. The client's plan to drive the generator with depth, edges, masks and pose therefore means either (a) using an open control model and accepting lower quality, or (b) feeding a **rendered skeleton video into a closed video-to-video model** (Aleph 2, Luma Modify "strong", Seedance 2.5 reference video, Gemini Omni editing outside the UK) and accepting looser structure. - Identity consistency through references is improving quickly (50 references in Seedance 2.5, Brand Kits in Runway). The product's *look* will increasingly come out right from generation alone. *Exactness* (legible small text, correct trademark geometry, exact colours) has no published measurement, so the checker and the composite-back step are still needed.

## 3. 3D and code-driven previs as the base for generation (Unreal, Unity, Blender, three.js → passes → video model)

### Takeaway
"Render a 3D scene, extract passes, let a generative model paint photoreal pixels" is an established research direction (2025 papers from NVIDIA, Kuaishou/Kling-affiliated authors, HKUST, CityU and others) and a **shipped open model in robotics and driving simulation** (NVIDIA Cosmos-Transfer, built for sim-to-real). ## 4. The client's approach compared: who else builds an exact skeleton first, generates on top with control inputs, measures clips against the plan, and composites exact elements back? Edge, niche or dead end?

### Takeaway
**The concept is not new.** Rendering an exact 3D/CG base and letting a generative model paint photoreal surfaces under depth, edge and segmentation control is the stated purpose of NVIDIA Cosmos-Transfer (sim-to-real), DiffusionRenderer (G-buffers → video), Diffusion as Shader (mesh-to-video) and I2V3D (coarse renders → video). Intangible sells a 3D-first, AI-render creative studio, and CG packshots composited over other footage is standard VFX practice. **What I did not find anywhere** is the full loop the client describes for ads and films: **pass marks written before the clip exists, an automatic checker that measures each generated clip against the skeleton, and exact parts composited back by default**. Research measures camera error and subject consistency after the fact, but not as a pre-registered production gate. That makes the approach a **real but narrow edge**: the parts are known, the assembly and the QA discipline are uncommon. Parts of it (identity consistency, readable text, camera from keyframes) are being taken over by the big models. Frame-accurate timing, exact logo pixels, exact colours, legal compliance and UK-specific model restrictions are not.

### Inferences
- **Common, niche or novel?** Each part is known: 3D previs is standard, CG packshot compositing is standard VFX, render-to-photoreal control is established research and a shipped robotics tool, and AI-3D studios exist (Intangible). The client's distinct choices are (1) **making the exact parts code-built and authoritative by default**, (2) **pre-registered pass marks with automatic measurement of every clip**, and (3) **composite-back as a rule, not an exception**. I found no product or studio publicly describing (2). So the approach is best described as a **niche, disciplined version of known VFX/sim-to-real practice**: not novel in concept, and uncommon in its QA rigour. Claiming it is "unique" would be wrong. Claiming it is "the only pipeline that measures itself against a plan written beforehand" was not contradicted by anything I found, but the search was cut short (see Gaps).
- **Dead end?** The part most at risk is "use structure control to get identity and camera right". References, keyframes and in-model 3D are improving quickly, and the top closed models already restyle a rendered video well. The part least at risk is **the checker plus composite-back of exact elements**. Brand-exact logos, legal text such as price, disclaimers and FSSAI/ASA-style claims, exact colours and frame-timed beats are outcomes a client is contractually held to. No vendor publishes accuracy for them, and the leaders' own docs show proportional timing and prompt-only camera.
- **Practical risk for the unproven step:** the best-quality models do not take depth or edge maps, and the models that do take them rank 200+ Elo lower (LTX-2.5 949 vs Wan 3.0 1174 on AA T2V). Expect the skin-on-skeleton step to work best as **render → closed video-to-video at strong adherence (Luma Modify, Aleph 2, Seedance reference video) → measure → composite exact layers**, with open control models (VACE, Cosmos-Transfer2.5) as a fallback where licences allow. For the UK market, avoid depending on Gemini Omni's uploaded-video editing and on MiniMax H3 weights.
- **Strategic reading:** the defensible value is less "we generate better" and more "we can prove the exact parts are exact and say what each clip passed". That is a QA and compliance service the platforms do not sell. It stays useful as long as brands are held to exact assets, which nothing read suggests will change.
